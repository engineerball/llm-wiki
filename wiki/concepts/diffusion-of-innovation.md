# Diffusion of Innovation

Framework (Everett Rogers, 1962) describing how new ideas and products spread through populations. [[Simon Sinek]] applies it to explain why [[Purpose-Driven Leadership]] targets believers, not the majority.

## The Five Adoption Groups

```
Innovators (2.5%) → Early Adopters (13.5%) → Early Majority (34%) → Late Majority (34%) → Laggards (16%)
```

| Group | % | Decision Driver |
|-------|---|-----------------|
| Innovators | 2.5% | Novelty; risk-tolerant; act on gut |
| Early Adopters | 13.5% | Belief alignment; "this reflects who I am" |
| Early Majority | 34% | Social proof; wait for trusted peers |
| Late Majority | 34% | Conformity; adopt when it's the norm |
| Laggards | 16% | Forced adoption or never |

## The Tipping Point

Innovators + Early Adopters combined = **16%** of any population. When adoption reaches this threshold, the idea gains momentum and crosses to the early majority automatically. This is Malcolm Gladwell's tipping point applied to product adoption.

Before the tipping point, growth requires active effort. After it, growth becomes self-sustaining.

The 16% threshold is the critical target. Sinek's prescription: ignore the mass market initially. Focus entirely on finding the Innovators and Early Adopters who already believe what you believe. They will recruit the rest.

## Sinek's Application

Most companies target the middle — early and late majority — because that's where the volume is. They use [[Manipulation vs Inspiration|manipulation]] (price, features, promotions) because those groups respond to rational cost-benefit.

But: early majority and late majority don't trust you enough to be first. They need social proof from early adopters first.

**The winning strategy**: win the 15–18% who share your beliefs. Don't market at the mass. Early adopters with shared beliefs become organic evangelists — they spread the message because it reflects their identity.

Paying early adopters or incentivizing them with gifts destroys this. Once they're compensated, they're no longer authentic believers — and they lose their influence over others.

## Why Belief Matters More Than Features

Early adopters don't buy because the product is the best. They buy because *it says something about who they are*. An [[Apple]] buyer in 2001 wasn't buying the best MP3 player; they were signaling that they think differently.

This is why [[Apple]] can move from computers to phones to watches to headphones. The product changes. The belief (empowering individuals to challenge the status quo) doesn't.
